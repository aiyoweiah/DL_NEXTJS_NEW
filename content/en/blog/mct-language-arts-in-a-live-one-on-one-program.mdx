There is a small library of language-arts books sitting on the shelves of many rigorous homeschooling families in North America. The covers are old-school: line drawings of grammar islands and sentence towns, thin softcovers with slightly dated typography. Together they make up **Michael Clay Thompson Language Arts**, published by Royal Fireworks Press — one of the most rigorous classical English language arts traditions in North America, and, for decades, one of the most quietly influential.

Parents who have found MCT tend to describe the same experience. Their child, for the first time, starts *seeing* how English is built. Not memorizing lists of words, not filling in blanks — noticing that *malevolent* holds *mal-* the way *benevolent* holds *bene-*, that a subordinate clause hooks onto a main clause the way a barnacle hooks onto a hull. A quiet click.

Then, often, the same thing happens next: the parent hits a wall.

Because the tradition itself is generous. It gives you Grammar Island for the first grammar map, Caesar's English for the roots, Paragraph Town for the writing arc, the Mud Trilogy for the classical literature. What it does not give you is the person on the other side of the table — someone who has read all of it, taught it many times, and knows what to ask *this* child, in *this* passage, when they say *"I don't know, it just sounded good."*

DODO Learning is a live, one-on-one English language arts program built on that gifted-ELA tradition. What follows is what actually changes for the student when the tradition is delivered live, by a trained Navigator, sixteen weeks at a time — and what the academic evidence says about why the core moves work.

## The tradition, and what it hands you

The MCT tradition rests on a few convictions that are unusual in modern school English:

- **Grammar is the operating system of writing.** Teach it first, teach it fast — cover *cover-to-cover* in the first weeks — then use it to analyze real sentences forever after.
- **Vocabulary is not a list. It is the *structure* of English.** English is a Latin-and-Greek language wearing a Germanic surface. Learn the roots and prefixes and suffixes, and thousands of words unlock as consequences.
- **Writing is a ladder.** Sentence to paragraph to essay to academic composition. Each rung is a real skill, and you climb them in order.
- **Reading is unabridged classics, one book at a time.** No simplified readers. No "high-interest, low-readability" workarounds. Whole texts, above the child's comfortable level, on purpose.
- **Poetry is not decorative.** It is the training of the *ear* — the sensitivity to how great sentences are actually built.

These convictions are almost dangerous to state as bullet points, because they land like slogans until you sit with a child and try to teach them. What happens then — every time — is that a bright ten-year-old opens *Alice in Wonderland* and says *"I don't get this part."* Not the plot. The part where the Queen of Hearts and the Duchess are arguing, and one of them says something whose surface is grammatically simple but whose *meaning* rests on a joke about Victorian propriety. And the child is stuck.

The tradition doesn't tell you what to say next.

## What live one-on-one adds — and why it matters

A Navigator sitting with that child does three things a book cannot:

**They ask a better question.** Not "what does this mean?" but "why does the Duchess care so much what the Queen calls the baby?" Not "was the pig a metaphor?" but "she said the baby was 'grunting'; did you notice she said it *before* it turned into a pig?" The Navigator's first move after any answer is always a more thought-provoking question — never a grade. This is a formal move, borrowed from Harvard's Project Zero Visible Thinking research. The point of a thinking routine is that it is *repeatable*: over a cycle, the child stops needing the Navigator to ask. They start asking themselves.

**They watch the writing come off the pen.** MCT's writing arc — Grammar → sentence → paragraph → essay → academic composition — is real, but the movement between rungs is where the ladder breaks. A Navigator sees the third comma splice on the second page and stops. Not with red ink. With a question: *"read this aloud — where does your voice want to break?"* The child hears it. Fluency, as writers know, is a decision about breath. It is not a rule to memorize.

**They defend an idea, live.** This is DODO's own strand — the Speaking pillar of the LCS System — and it has no equivalent in a curriculum of books. A twelve-year-old argues in real time that Frederick Douglass is *not* only writing to abolitionists, that the second-person address in a specific paragraph is aimed at a slaveholder who has convinced himself he is decent. A Navigator asks: *"where in the text?"* The child finds the sentence. Then: *"why that verb?"* The child re-reads. Something opens.

That specific kind of thinking — the interior act of forming a position and then *defending* it in words, with a person who takes it seriously — is what a book on a shelf cannot produce. It is also, not coincidentally, what the SSAT, IB Language & Literature, and every university admissions essay eventually ask for.

## What the research actually says

DODO's vocabulary and reading practice sit on one of the most-researched strategies in education. The evidence is dated and specific:

- **Gallagher (2017)** studied 493 middle-school students across 10 teachers using *The Word Within the Word* — the MCT vocabulary tradition's core text. Root-based vocabulary teaching **outperformed traditional instruction** on sentence completion in Grades 6 and 7, and produced **moderate-to-large effects on recall at every grade level** — for both gifted and typically-developing students.
- **Goodwin and Ahn (2010, 2013)** ran two meta-analyses, one across 17 studies and one across 30. Both found consistent effects for teaching the *structure* of words: overall literacy gains of *d = 0.33* and *d = 0.32*, vocabulary *d = 0.40* and *d = 0.34*, decoding *d = 0.59* in the larger review.
- **Bowers, Kirby, and Deacon (2010)** reviewed 22 studies and found the effect is strongest when morphological instruction is **woven into other literacy work**, not delivered as isolated drills. That validates the integrated model DODO uses — Latin roots meeting an unabridged classic in the same sentence, both anchored by grammar and both delivered live.
- **Henry (1997)** — the famous number — found that about **60% of English text is Latin or Greek in origin**; over **90% in science and technology writing**. When we say a child who has learned roots holds a key to the academic vocabulary of every subject, we mean it literally.

Two things about that citation list are worth naming.

The first is that most of this evidence was originally studied on populations that included ELL learners, struggling readers, and students with dyslexia. That is where the research had funding. **This is not how DODO teaches.** DODO's frame is *acceleration into mastery* — the same evidence, deployed to help capable students go further. The interventions that work with children who are behind also, it turns out, work with children who are ahead. They work with anyone who is learning to read a language whose engine is Latin and Greek.

The second is that this level of citation is not typical of English-tutoring marketing. Most programs describe outcomes with adjectives. DODO does not, because we don't have to. The tradition we teach is one of the most-studied strategies in education — and it is one of the few places where "we chose this because the evidence says so" is a simple, verifiable statement.

## Measured, not asserted

The tradition gives DODO its content and its methodology. What DODO adds around the tradition is a live Navigator delivering it, and a measurement layer that shows what changed:

- **Reading is measured in Lexile.** Every ELA Program student gets a baseline Lexile at Week 0, a midpoint check at Week 8, and an exit measurement at Week 16 — administered by their own Navigator with MetaMetrics-certified tools. A typical Lexile gain is 187L on average per 16-week cycle; the canonical claim is *about one full grade level of reading per cycle*.
- **Writing is measured on the 6+1 Trait rubric.** Ideas, Organization, Voice, Word Choice, Sentence Fluency, Conventions, Presentation — evaluated per session. Movement is reported per-trait; parents can see specifically which dimensions have moved. Average 2× gain from entry to exit, with the largest movements typically in Ideas, Organization, and Voice.

The measurement is not the point. The measurement is what makes the tradition *provable*. A parent enrolling a bright ten-year-old on the belief that the classical route will produce depth and it will be visible — the numbers are the receipt.

## Who this is actually for

The tradition of MCT was not built for children learning English as a second language. It was built for gifted and advanced learners — the kind of child who reads *Alice in Wonderland* on their own at nine and could technically pass the school English test but knows, somewhere, that they are being *underspent*. The child who is fluent enough in English that no one at school notices the gap, but the gap is real: they can write a paragraph, they cannot yet argue a position; they can summarize a chapter, they cannot yet interpret a symbol.

That gap is what DODO is built to close. Live, one child at a time, sixteen weeks per arc, measured every eight weeks, over as many arcs as it takes to reach mastery.

If the child in question is yours, and you have wondered whether the shelf of MCT books at home is enough — the honest answer is that they are extraordinary raw material, and they are not enough. The person on the other side of the table is what turns the tradition into growth.
